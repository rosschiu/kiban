    Module inside or app beside¶

    Teams arriving from an out-of-process authorizer read that Kiban writes a grant in the same transaction as the audit row and conclude they can drop their own consistency layer. That is true for one of the two shapes an integration can take, and not for the other. This page says which, what each gives up, and the pattern for the common case.

    The two shapes¶

    Module inside Kiban App beside Kiban
    Where it runs Compiled into the platform, routed by the gateway, its schema in Kiban's Postgres Its own process, its own database, any language
    How it writes a grant A SQL write in the same transaction as its own row and the audit event POST /api/auth/grants over HTTP, after or before its own commit
    Consistency with its own data Atomic: the row and the tuple exist together or not at all Two systems, two commits: a failure between them leaves one without the other
    What it gives up Any language but Go; a release cycle tied to Kiban's; docs/building.md's contract Atomicity between its rows and its tuples
    Who should pick it A contributor extending the platform, the four samples Almost every application

    The four sample modules are the first shape. Integrate your app is the second, and it is the one to choose unless you are extending Kiban itself.

    What "same transaction" covers, and what it does not¶

    Inside Kiban, every mutation and its audit event commit together, and a module's grant is one of those mutations. The guarantee is between Kiban's own tables.

    An app beside Kiban still gets that guarantee for Kiban's side: a grant request either writes every tuple it names and the ledger rows, or nothing. What Kiban cannot promise is anything about your database. Your transaction and the grant request are two commits, and the process can die between them.

    The pattern for an app beside Kiban¶

    Two facts make this simple:

    • A grant is idempotent. Granting a tuple that already exists writes nothing and answers 200; the same for revoking one that is gone. Repeating a grant is always safe.
    • A check denies while the tuple is missing. A row without its tuple is invisible to its intended readers, never visible to the wrong ones. So the safe order is to make the tuple exist before the row is reachable, and to clean the tuple up after the row is gone.

    Creating an object:

    1. Choose the object id yourself (a UUID), before anything is written.
    2. Grant the anchor and the first relations in one call, with that id.
    3. Commit your own row.

    If step 3 fails, Kiban holds tuples for an object that does not exist. They grant access to nothing, since there is no row to serve. Revoke the anchor when you notice, or leave them for the sweep below; anchoring the same id again starts from nothing either way.

    If the process dies between 2 and 3, the same applies. If it dies before 2 completes, nothing exists on either side.

    Deleting an object:

    1. Delete your row, or mark it deleted.
    2. Revoke the anchor. Every other tuple on the object goes with it in the same transaction.

    If step 2 fails, the tuples outlive the row; they grant access to nothing. The sweep removes them.

    Granting and revoking a share on an existing object:

    • Grant first, then record the share in your database if you keep a copy. A failure after the grant leaves access that your UI does not show; the sweep below reconciles it.
    • For a revoke, record the removal first, then revoke. A failure after the record leaves access the UI no longer shows, until the sweep.

    The reconciliation sweep:

    A periodic job walks your rows and re-issues the grants they imply, and revokes the anchors of ids your database no longer has. Because grants are idempotent, the job can run as often as you like and needs no state of its own: for each row, grant(anchor + relations); for each anchor you know you created and whose row is gone, revoke(anchor). Keep the set of ids you created somewhere, a column or a log, so the job knows what to revoke. The sweep is the whole consistency layer; it replaces nothing you would have needed for Kiban's own side.

    If you keep no copy of shares in your database and treat Kiban as the only truth for who may see what, the sweep reduces to the delete case: revoke anchors of ids that no longer exist.

    What to tell a reviewer¶

    "Kiban is consistent with itself in one transaction. Our rows and Kiban's tuples are two commits; we grant before the row exists and revoke after it is gone, so a crash in between leaves tuples that grant nothing, and a sweep re-issues idempotent grants and revokes orphan anchors." That is the honest sentence; it is shorter than the consistency layer it replaces.
